## Basic Concepts of Probability

$$\boxed{\enspace \text{Probability} = \frac{\text{Favorable Outcomes}}{\text{Total Outcomes}} \enspace}$$

* **Random Experiment:** An experiment whose exact outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance. For example: tossing a coin (Coin flip), throwing a die.
* **Sample Space ($S$):** The set of all possible outcomes (results) of a single random experiment.
  * Tossing a coin (Coin flip) $\longrightarrow S = \{H, T\}$
  * Throwing a die $\longrightarrow S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$

### Types of Events

1. **Simple & Compound Events:**
   Suppose a bag contains 1 white ($1W$) and 2 black ($2B$) balls.
   * **Simple Event:** An event consisting of only a single sample point/element (can occur in only one way). For example: the event of drawing a white ball.
   * **Compound Event:** An event consisting of more than one sample point/element (can occur in multiple ways). For example: the event of drawing a black ball.

2. **Mutually Exclusive Events:**
   If there is no common element between two events, they are called mutually exclusive events. That is, $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
   * **Example 1:** Tossing a coin $\{H, T\}$ and throwing a die $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ (No common elements).
   * **Example 2:** The events of getting $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{4, 5, 6\}$ in a throw of a die.

3. **Non-Mutually Exclusive Events:**
   When there is at least one common element between two events.

![[assets/non_mutually_exclusive_dice.png]] [Diagram: Common elements {4, 6} between even numbers {2, 4, 6} and numbers greater than 3 {4, 5, 6} in throwing a die]

4. **Independent Events:**
   If the occurrence or non-occurrence of one event does not affect the occurrence of another event (i.e., neither depends on the other), they are called independent events.

5. **Dependent Events:**
   The opposite of independent events; that is, when the occurrence of one event is dependent on another event.

6. **Certain Event and Impossible Event:**
   * **Certain (Sure) Event:** An event that is guaranteed to occur ($P = 1$).
   * **Impossible Event:** An event that can never occur ($P = 0$).

7. **Complementary Event:**
   All the distinct mutually exclusive outcomes that can occur in an experiment are complementary to each other. If an event is $A$, its complementary event is denoted by $A^c$ or $A'$.

> [!note] Properties of Probability
> 1. The probability of any event always lies between $0$ and $1$:
>    $$\boxed{\enspace 0 \le P(A) \le 1 \enspace} \qquad \text{or,} \qquad 0\% \le P(A) \le 100\%$$
> 2. If the probability of an event occurring is $P$, then the **probability of the event not occurring** $= 1 - P$.

![[assets/coin_flip_tree_diagram.png]] [Diagram: Pencil tree diagram (Probability Tree) for 3 successive coin tosses]

Sample space,
$$S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\} \qquad [\text{Total outcomes, } n(S) = 8]$$

* **Probability of getting $H$ and $T$ alternately:** $\frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}$ (favorable outcomes: $HTH, THT$)

## Combination & Counting Problems

> [!important] 01: Find the indicated probability in each of the following cases:
> (i) A box contains $7R, 9B, 6W$ balls. If 1 ball is drawn at random, what is the probability that it is red or white, $P(R \text{ or } W) = ?$
> (ii) A box contains $8R, 4B, 3W$ balls. If 3 balls are drawn at random, what is the probability that exactly 2 are red, $P(2R) = ?$
> (iii) A box contains $5R, 10W$ balls. If 2 balls are drawn, what is the probability that they are (a) of different colors and (b) of the same color?
> (iv) A box contains $7R, 5W$ balls. If 4 balls are drawn, what is the probability that 2 are white and 2 are red, $P(2W \text{ \& } 2R) = ?$
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > **(i)** Total balls $= 7 + 9 + 6 = 22$.
> > 
> > $$
> > P(R \text{ or } W) = \frac{7 + 6}{7 + 9 + 6} = \boxed{\frac{13}{22}}
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(ii)** Total balls $= 8 + 4 + 3 = 15$. There are 8 red balls and $(4 + 3) = 7$ balls of other colors.
> > * Choosing 2 balls from 8 red balls $= {}^8C_2$
> > * Choosing the remaining 1 ball from the other 7 balls $= {}^7C_1$
> > * Choosing 3 balls from a total of 15 balls $= {}^{15}C_3$
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P(2R) = \boxed{\frac{{}^8C_2 \times {}^7C_1}{{}^{15}C_3}}
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(iii)** Total balls $= 5 + 10 = 15$.
> > * **(a) Probability of being different colors:** 1 red and 1 white ball will be drawn,
> >   $$P(\text{diff color}) = \boxed{\frac{{}^5C_1 \times {}^{10}C_1}{{}^{15}C_2}}$$
> > * **(b) Probability of being the same color:** Using the rule of complementary events,
> >   $$P(\text{same color}) = 1 - P(\text{diff color}) = \boxed{1 - \frac{{}^5C_1 \times {}^{10}C_1}{{}^{15}C_2}}$$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(iv)** Total balls $= 7 + 5 = 12$. Probability of getting 2 red and 2 white balls out of 4 balls:
> > 
> > $$
> > P(2W \text{ \& } 2R) = \boxed{\frac{{}^7C_2 \times {}^5C_2}{{}^{12}C_4}}
> > $$

> [!important] 02: Out of 200 students, 40 failed in Mathematics, 20 failed in Statistics, and 10 failed in both subjects. If a student is selected at random, what is the probability that they failed in Mathematics only (and not in Statistics)?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Total students, $n(S) = 200$.
> > 40 failed in Mathematics and 10 failed in both subjects.
> > Therefore, failed in Mathematics only $= 40 - 10 = 30$ students.
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P(\text{Failed in Math only, not in Statistics}) = \frac{40 - 10}{200} = \frac{30}{200} = \boxed{\frac{3}{20}}
> > $$

## Addition Law of Probability

![[assets/venn_diagram_addition_law.png]] [Diagram: Two non-mutually exclusive events A and B inside sample space S and their intersection A ∩ B]

* $\cap \text{ (Intersection)} \longrightarrow \text{Common} \longrightarrow \text{And Operation (both / together)}$
* $\cup \text{ (Union)} \longrightarrow \text{Total} \longrightarrow \text{Or Operation (either / or)}$

### 1. For Non-Mutually Exclusive Events
$$\boxed{\enspace P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof
> Let,
> * Event $A$ can occur in $= n(A)$ ways
> * Event $B$ can occur in $= n(B)$ ways
> * Total outcomes in the sample space $= n(S)$
> 
> By the addition law of sets,
> 
> $$
> n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)
> $$
> 
> Dividing both sides by $n(S)$, we get,
> 
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \frac{n(A \cup B)}{n(S)} &= \frac{n(A)}{n(S)} + \frac{n(B)}{n(S)} - \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(S)} \\
> \Rightarrow P(A \cup B) &= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
> \end{aligned}
> $$

### 2. For Mutually Exclusive Events
In mutually exclusive events, there is nothing in common, so $P(A \cap B) = 0$.
$$\boxed{\enspace P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \enspace}$$
> **Summary:** In the case of **"or"**, the total probability of mutually exclusive events is the **sum** of their individual probabilities.

## Multiplication Law of Probability & Conditional Probability

* $P(A/B) \longrightarrow$ The probability of event $A$ occurring given the condition that event $B$ has already happened.
* $P(B/A) \longrightarrow$ The probability of event $B$ occurring given the condition that event $A$ has already happened.

$$\boxed{\enspace P(A/B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \enspace} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \boxed{\enspace P(A \cap B) = P(A/B) \cdot P(B) = P(B/A) \cdot P(A) \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof (Derivation with Example)
> **Example:** Out of 200 boys, 40 play Football ($B$), 20 play Cricket ($A$), and 10 play both games ($A \cap B$). A boy is selected at random and it is found that he plays Football. What is the probability that he also plays Cricket?
> 
> * Since we already know that the boy plays Football (event $B$ has already happened), he must be one of those 40 boys. Thus, the new **total outcomes** here $= n(B) = 40$.
> * Now, for him to play Cricket, he must fall into the "Both" category. Thus, **favorable outcomes** $= n(A \cap B) = 10$.
> 
> Therefore, the probability of playing Cricket given that he plays Football is:
> 
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> P(A/B) &= \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)} \\
> &= \frac{\frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(S)}}{\frac{n(B)}{n(S)}} \qquad [\text{Dividing numerator and denominator by } n(S)] \\
> &= \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> 
> Cross-multiplying, we get,
> 
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A/B) \cdot P(B)
> $$
> 
> Similarly, since $A \cap B = B \cap A$, we have $P(B/A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \implies P(A \cap B) = P(B/A) \cdot P(A)$.

### Multiplication Law for Independent Events
If $A$ and $B$ are mutually independent, whether $B$ has occurred or not does not affect $A$. Consequently:
$$P(A/B) = P(A) \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(B/A) = P(B)$$

Substituting this into the multiplication law gives:
$$\boxed{\enspace P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \enspace}$$

> [!note] Summary: When to Use the Addition vs. Multiplication Law?
> * **Mutually Exclusive Events (Either / Or $\rightarrow$ Addition):** When an event has multiple mutually exclusive cases or alternatives (e.g., if two balls are drawn, the probability of *both being black* **or** *both being white*), they share no common elements ($P(A \cap B) = 0$). In this case, the total probability is the **sum** of the individual probabilities:
>   $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$
> * **Independent Events (And $\rightarrow$ Multiplication):** When one event does not depend on another (e.g., drawing two balls successively from two different bags), the total probability of both events occurring together (**and**) is the **product** of their individual probabilities:
>   $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$

## Application of Complementary Events (Idea of Complement)

If the probability of an event $A$ occurring is $P(A)$ and the probability of it not occurring (complement) is $P(A^c)$, then:
$$\boxed{\enspace P(A) = 1 - P(A^c) \enspace}$$
*(To find the probability of "at least one" event occurring, the easiest method is to subtract the probability of "none occurring" from $1$)*.

> [!important] 03: The 1st bag contains $5W, 3B$ balls and the 2nd bag contains $4W, 5B$ balls. If 1 ball is drawn from each bag, what is the probability that **at least one is White**?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Here, total balls in the 1st bag $= 5 + 3 = 8$ and total balls in the 2nd bag $= 4 + 5 = 9$.
> > 
> > **Method 1 (Using Complement - Easier Method):**
> > Having no white balls means **both are black ($BB$)**:
> > 
> > $$
> > P(\text{all black}) = P(BB) = P(B_1) \cdot P(B_2) = \frac{3}{8} \times \frac{5}{9} = \frac{15}{72}
> > $$
> > 
> > Therefore, the probability of getting at least 1 white ball is,
> > 
> > $$
> > P(\text{at least 1 } W) = 1 - P(BB) = 1 - \frac{15}{72} = \boxed{\frac{57}{72}}
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **Method 2 (Normal Process):**
> > At least 1 white ball can occur in 3 ways: ($1\text{st } W, 2\text{nd } B$) or ($1\text{st } B, 2\text{nd } W$) or ($1\text{st } W, 2\text{nd } W$).
> > 
> > $$
> > \begin{aligned}
> > P(\text{at least 1 } W) &= P(WB) + P(BW) + P(WW) \\
> > &= P(W_1)\cdot P(B_2) + P(B_1)\cdot P(W_2) + P(W_1)\cdot P(W_2) \\
> > &= \left(\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{5}{9}\right) + \left(\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{4}{9}\right) + \left(\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{4}{9}\right) \\
> > &= \frac{25 + 12 + 20}{72} = \boxed{\frac{57}{72}}
> > \end{aligned}
> > $$

> [!important] 04: Three boxes contain $(2W, 3B)$, $(3W, 4B)$, and $(4W, 5B)$ balls respectively. If 1 ball is drawn from each box, what is the probability of getting **at least 1 white ($W$)** ball?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > * **Normal Process:** 7 distinct cases must be added—
> >   $$P(\text{at least 1 } W) = P(WBB) + P(WWB) + P(WWW) + P(BWB) + P(BWW) + P(BBW) + P(WBW)$$
> > 
> > * **Complement Process (Easier Method):**
> >   Subtracting the probability of getting no white balls from the three boxes—i.e., all three being black ($BBB$)—from $1$ gives the probability of getting at least 1 white ball:
> >   
> >   $$
> >   \begin{aligned}
> >   P(\text{at least 1 } W) &= 1 - P(BBB) \\
> >   &= 1 - \left(\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{4}{7} \times \frac{5}{9}\right) \\
> >   &= 1 - \frac{4}{21} = \boxed{\frac{17}{21}}
> >   \end{aligned}
> >   $$

> [!important] 05: The number of job applicants in three offices $a, b, c$ are $4, 5, 6$ respectively. If a candidate applies to all three offices, what is the probability of **getting at least one job**?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Assuming 1 person is selected in each office:
> > 
> > | Office | Total Applicants | Probability of Getting the Job | Probability of Not Getting the Job |
> > | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> > | $a$ | $4$ | $\frac{1}{4}$ | $1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$ |
> > | $b$ | $5$ | $\frac{1}{5}$ | $1 - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{4}{5}$ |
> > | $c$ | $6$ | $\frac{1}{6}$ | $1 - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{5}{6}$ |
> > 
> > Probability of not getting a job in any of the offices,
> > 
> > $$
> > P(XXX) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{4}{5} \times \frac{5}{6} = \frac{1}{2}
> > $$
> > 
> > Therefore, the probability of getting at least one job is,
> > 
> > $$
> > P = 1 - P(XXX) = 1 - \left(\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{4}{5} \times \frac{5}{6}\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \boxed{\frac{1}{2}}
> > $$

> [!important] 06: Observing the resistors in each of the 3 parallel branches of the electrical circuit below shows that connection is established for $1\text{ hr}$ out of $7\text{ hr}$, and for the remaining time ($6\text{ hr}$) it remains an open circuit. What is the probability of getting current flow in the main circuit?
> ![[assets/parallel_circuit_probability.png]] [Diagram: Circuit with 3 resistors connected in parallel to a voltage source]
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > In each branch, the probability of the resistor conducting (connection established) $= \frac{1}{7}$, and the probability of remaining an open circuit (no current flow) $= \frac{6}{7}$.
> > 
> > In a parallel circuit, main current ($I$) will fail to flow only when **all three branches are open simultaneously**.
> > * Probability of no current flow ($I$), $P(XXX) = \frac{6}{7} \cdot \frac{6}{7} \cdot \frac{6}{7} = \frac{6^3}{7^3}$
> > * Therefore, the probability of getting current flow ($I$) in the circuit is:
> >   $$P = \boxed{1 - \frac{6^3}{7^3}} = 1 - \frac{216}{343} = \frac{127}{343}$$

## Number-Related Problems

> [!important] 07: If an integer is chosen at random from $20$ to $520$ inclusive, what is the probability that it is an **odd cube number**?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Total numbers from $20$ to $520$,
> > 
> > $$
> > n(S) = (520 - 20) + 1 = 501
> > $$
> > 
> > The perfect cube numbers within this range are:
> > * $3^3 = \mathbf{27}$ (Odd)
> > * $4^3 = 64$ (Even)
> > * $5^3 = \mathbf{125}$ (Odd)
> > * $6^3 = 216$ (Even)
> > * $7^3 = \mathbf{343}$ (Odd)
> > * $8^3 = 512$ (Even)
> > 
> > Therefore, there are $3$ odd cube numbers ($27, 125, 343$).
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore \text{Probability} = \frac{3}{501} = \boxed{\frac{1}{167}}
> > $$

> [!important] 08: If a number is chosen at random from $1$ to $20$, what is the probability that it is a multiple of $3$ or $5$?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Here, total numbers, $n(S) = 20$.
> > * Multiples of $3$, $A = \{3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18\} \implies n(A) = 6$
> > * Multiples of $5$, $B = \{5, 10, 15, 20\} \implies n(B) = 4$
> > * Common multiples of both, $A \cap B = \{15\} \implies n(A \cap B) = 1$
> > 
> > By the addition law of probability,
> > 
> > $$
> > \begin{aligned}
> > P(A \cup B) &= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \\
> > &= \frac{6}{20} + \frac{4}{20} - \frac{1}{20} = \boxed{\frac{9}{20}}
> > \end{aligned}
> > $$

> [!important] 09: If a number is chosen at random from the natural numbers from $1$ to $100$, find the probability that it satisfies the following conditions:
> (i) Probability that the number is prime
> (ii) Probability that it is prime or divisible by $5$
> (iii) Probability that it is divisible by both $2$ and $3$
> (iv) Probability that it satisfies the condition $|x - 4| < 13$
> (v) Probability that it satisfies the condition $x + \frac{100}{x} \le 50$
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Here, total numbers in the sample space, $n(S) = 100$.
> > 
> > **(i) Prime number:**
> > There are $25$ prime numbers from $1$ to $100$.
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P(\text{Prime}) = \frac{25}{100} = \boxed{\frac{1}{4}}
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(ii) Prime or divisible by $5$:**
> > * Prime numbers $= 25$
> > * Numbers divisible by $5 = \frac{100}{5} = 20$
> > * Again, $5$ itself is a prime number that is common to both sets ($1$ number).
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P = \frac{25}{100} + \frac{20}{100} - \frac{1}{100} = \frac{44}{100} = \boxed{\frac{11}{25}}
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(iii) Divisible by both $2$ and $3$:**
> > Being divisible by both $2$ and $3$ simultaneously means being divisible by $(2 \times 3) = 6$.
> > * $\frac{100}{6} = 16.66 \implies$ As integers, a total of $16$ numbers are divisible by $6$.
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P = \frac{16}{100} = \boxed{\frac{4}{25}}
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(iv) For the condition $|x - 4| < 13$:**
> > 
> > $$
> > -13 < x - 4 < 13 \implies -9 < x < 17
> > $$
> > 
> > Since the number lies between $1$ and $100$, the acceptable natural numbers are from $1$ to $16$ (or up to $17$ if the inequality is $\le$).
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P = \boxed{\frac{16}{100}} \quad \left[\text{Taking } 17 \text{ numbers as noted in the notebook, } P = \frac{17}{100}\right]
> > $$
> > 
> > ---
> > **(v) For the condition $x + \frac{100}{x} \le 50$:**
> > 
> > $$
> > \begin{aligned}
> > x + \frac{100}{x} &\le 50 \\
> > \Rightarrow x^2 - 50x + 100 &\le 0 \\
> > \Rightarrow (x - 47.92)(x - 2.08) &\le 0 \\
> > \Rightarrow 2.08 \le x &\le 47.92
> > \end{aligned}
> > $$
> > 
> > Therefore, the integers lying within this interval are from $3$ to $47$, which is a total of $(47 - 3 + 1) = 45$ numbers.
> > 
> > $$
> > \therefore P = \frac{45}{100} = \boxed{\frac{9}{20}}
> > $$
