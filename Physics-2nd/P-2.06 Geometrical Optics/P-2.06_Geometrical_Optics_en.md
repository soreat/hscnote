## Fermat's Principle

> When light travels from one point to another after being reflected or refracted at a transparent surface, it always follows the path of minimum time (shortest optical path).

#### Proof of Reflection:
![[assets/fermat_reflection.png]] [Diagram: Proof of reflection using Fermat's principle]

> [!tip]- Proof
> From the figure, 
> $$S_1 = \sqrt{h_1^2 + x^2}$$
> $$S_2 = \sqrt{h_2^2 + (l-x)^2}$$
> 
> Total time, $t = t_1 + t_2 \Rightarrow t = \frac{S_1}{c} + \frac{S_2}{c}$
> 
> $$
> t = \frac{\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}}{c} + \frac{\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}}{c}
> $$
> 
> According to Fermat's principle, for minimum time, $\frac{dt}{dx} = 0$
> 
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{1}{c} \cdot \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}} + \frac{1}{c} \cdot \frac{2(l-x)(-1)}{2\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}} = 0
> $$
> 
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{x}{c\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}} = \frac{l-x}{c\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}}
> $$
> 
> From the figure, 
> $$ \sin i = \frac{x}{\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}} $$ 
> $$ \sin r = \frac{l-x}{\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}} $$
> 
> Therefore,
> $$ \frac{\sin i}{c} = \frac{\sin r}{c} $$
> $$ \Rightarrow \sin i = \sin r $$
> $$ \boxed{\enspace \therefore i = r \enspace} $$
> (Law of reflection proved)

#### Proof of Refraction:
![[assets/fermat_refraction.png]] [Diagram: Proof of refraction using Fermat's principle]

> [!tip]- Proof
> From the figure, 
> $$S_1 = \sqrt{h_1^2 + x^2}$$
> $$S_2 = \sqrt{h_2^2 + (l-x)^2}$$
> 
> If the velocity of light in medium $a$ is $c_a$ and in medium $b$ is $c_b$, then total time:
> 
> $$
> t = t_1 + t_2 = \frac{S_1}{c_a} + \frac{S_2}{c_b}
> $$
> 
> $$
> t = \frac{\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}}{c_a} + \frac{\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}}{c_b}
> $$
> 
> According to Fermat's principle, $\frac{dt}{dx} = 0$
> 
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{1}{c_a} \cdot \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}} + \frac{1}{c_b} \cdot \frac{2(l-x)(-1)}{2\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}} = 0
> $$
> 
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{x}{c_a\sqrt{h_1^2+x^2}} = \frac{l-x}{c_b\sqrt{h_2^2+(l-x)^2}}
> $$
> 
> Substituting the values from the figure, we get,
> 
> $$
> \frac{\sin i}{c_a} = \frac{\sin r}{c_b}
> $$
> 
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{c_a}{c_b}
> $$
> 
> We know, $\frac{c_a}{c_b} = {}_a\mu_b$, therefore:
> $$ \boxed{\enspace {}_a\mu_b = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} \enspace} $$
> (Law of refraction proved)


## Refraction of Light
> Angle of incidence $\neq$ Angle of refraction
> $$i \neq r$$
> The phenomenon of bending of a light ray at the interface (surface of separation) of two media when it travels obliquely from one transparent medium to another is called refraction of light.

![[assets/refraction.png]] [Diagram: Refraction of light]


**Laws of Refraction:**
1. The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal drawn at the point of incidence on the interface of the two media all lie in the same plane.
2. For a given pair of media and for light of a specific color (monochromatic light), the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant. (Snell's Law)
$$
\frac{\sin i_1}{\sin r_1} = \frac{\sin i_2}{\sin r_2} = \frac{\sin i_3}{\sin r_3} = \dots = \mu \text{ (Refractive Index)}
$$

### Refractive Index
Refractive index is of 2 types:
1. **Relative Refractive Index:** When a ray of light travels obliquely from one medium to another, for a given pair of media and light of a specific color, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is called the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium.
   * Refractive index of medium $b$ with respect to medium $a$, $\boxed{ {}_a\mu_b = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} }$
   * Refractive index of medium $a$ with respect to medium $b$, $\boxed{ {}_b\mu_a = \frac{\sin r}{\sin i} = \frac{1}{{}_a\mu_b} }$
2. **Absolute Refractive Index:** When a ray of light travels obliquely from a vacuum into another medium, for light of a specific color, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is called the absolute refractive index of that medium.
   * Absolute refractive index of medium $a$, $\boxed{ {}_0\mu_a = \mu_a = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} }$

#### Refraction of Light through a Medium Bounded by Parallel Surfaces
![[assets/parallel_plates_refraction.png]] [Diagram: Refraction through parallel surfaces]

Refractive index of medium $b$ with respect to medium $a$, ${}_a\mu_b = \frac{\sin i_1}{\sin r_1} \dots (i)$
Refractive index of medium $c$ with respect to medium $b$, ${}_b\mu_c = \frac{\sin r_1}{\sin r_2} \dots (ii)$
Refractive index of medium $a$ with respect to medium $c$, ${}_c\mu_a = \frac{\sin r_2}{\sin i_1} \dots (iii)$ (Since the angle of emergence is $i_1$)

From $(i) \times (ii) \times (iii)$, we get,
$$
{}_a\mu_b \times {}_b\mu_c \times {}_c\mu_a = \frac{\sin i_1}{\sin r_1} \times \frac{\sin r_1}{\sin r_2} \times \frac{\sin r_2}{\sin i_1}
$$
$$
\boxed{\enspace {}_a\mu_b \times {}_b\mu_c \times {}_c\mu_a = 1 \enspace}
$$

From this, the relation between relative and absolute refractive indices is obtained:
$$
{}_a\mu_b = \frac{1}{{}_b\mu_c \times {}_c\mu_a} \Rightarrow {}_a\mu_b = \frac{{}_0\mu_b}{{}_0\mu_a} = \frac{\mu_b}{\mu_a}
$$

#### Relation between Refractive Index, Velocity of Light, and Wavelength
$$
\boxed{\enspace {}_a\mu_b = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{\mu_b}{\mu_a} = \frac{c_a}{c_b} = \frac{\lambda_a}{\lambda_b} \enspace}
$$
> [!note] 
> $\text{Refractive index} \propto \text{Optical density} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Velocity of light}}$
> That is, the higher the refractive index of a medium, the lower the velocity of light in that medium.
 
> [!important] 01: At what angle of incidence must a ray of light strike a medium of refractive index $1.62$ so that the reflected ray and the refracted ray are perpendicular to each other?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > According to the figure, the angle between the reflected ray and the refracted ray is $90^\circ$.
> > Therefore, $r = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - i = 90^\circ - i$
> > 
> > From Snell's law,
> > $$ \mu = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} $$
> > $$ \mu = \frac{\sin i}{\sin(90^\circ - i)} $$
> > $$ \mu = \frac{\sin i}{\cos i} = \tan i $$
> > 
> > Given, $\mu = 1.62$
> > $$ \tan i = 1.62 \Rightarrow i = \tan^{-1}(1.62) $$
> > $$ \therefore i = 58.31^\circ $$

> [!important] 02: When a ray of light emerges from water into a vacuum, it undergoes an angular deviation of $30^\circ$. If the refractive index of water is $\frac{4}{3}$, what is the angle of incidence?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Light is traveling from a denser medium (water) to a rarer medium (vacuum), so it bends away from the normal. 
> > That is, the angle of refraction is $r = i + 30^\circ$.
> > 
> > From the general form of Snell's law,
> > $$ \mu_w \sin i = \mu_a \sin r $$
> > $$ \frac{4}{3} \sin i = 1 \cdot \sin(i + 30^\circ) $$
> > 
> > Inputting $\frac{4}{3} = \frac{\sin X}{\sin(X + 30^\circ)}$ directly into the calculator,
> > $$ i = 46.93^\circ $$

> [!important] 03: The refractive index of glass is $1.5$ and that of water is $1.33$. If a ray of light is incident at an angle of $30^\circ$ while traveling from glass into water, what is the angular deviation?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > From Snell's law,
> > $$ \mu_g \sin i = \mu_w \sin r $$
> > $$ 1.5 \sin 30^\circ = 1.33 \sin r $$
> > $$ \Rightarrow r = 34.32^\circ $$
> > 
> > Angular deviation, $\delta = r - i$ (since $r > i$)
> > $$ \delta = 34.32^\circ - 30^\circ = 4.32^\circ $$

> [!important] 04: The refractive index of glass with respect to heavy water ($D_2O$) is $0.85$. The refractive index of water with respect to heavy water is $1.2$. What is the refractive index of glass with respect to water?
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Given,
> > ${}_D\mu_g = 0.85$
> > ${}_D\mu_w = 1.2$
> > To find, ${}_w\mu_g = ?$
> > 
> > We know,
> > $$ {}_w\mu_g = \frac{\mu_g}{\mu_w} = \frac{\mu_g/\mu_D}{\mu_w/\mu_D} = \frac{{}_D\mu_g}{{}_D\mu_w} $$
> > $$ {}_w\mu_g = \frac{0.85}{1.2} = 0.708 $$


### Critical Angle and Total Internal Reflection

>**Critical Angle:** When a ray of light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium, the specific angle of incidence for which the refracted ray grazes along the interface of the two media—meaning the angle of refraction becomes $90^\circ$—is called the critical angle. It is denoted by $\theta_c$.

![[assets/critical_angle.png]] [Diagram: Critical angle and total internal reflection]

>**Total Internal Reflection:** When a ray of light traveling from a denser medium to a rarer medium is incident at an angle greater than the critical angle, instead of being refracted, the light ray is completely reflected back into the previous (denser) medium from the interface of the two media. This phenomenon is called total internal reflection.

**Conditions:**
1. The ray of light must travel from a denser medium to a rarer medium.
2. The angle of incidence in the denser medium must be greater than the critical angle ($i > \theta_c$). 
(In the case of total internal reflection, the laws of reflection apply, i.e., $i = r$)

**Relation between Refractive Index and Critical Angle:**
According to Snell's law (from denser medium to rarer medium),
$$ \mu \sin \theta_c = \mu_o \cdot \sin 90^\circ $$
$$ \mu \sin \theta_c = \mu_o $$
$$ \boxed{\enspace \sin \theta_c = \frac{\mu_o}{\mu} \enspace} $$
*(where $\mu_o$ is the refractive index of the rarer medium and $\mu$ is the refractive index of the denser medium)*


> [!important] 01: At what angle (with the vertical) must a fish look from under water to see the setting sun? (Refractive index of water is $\frac{4}{3}$)
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > $$ \sin \theta_c = \frac{1}{\mu} $$
> > $$ \sin \theta_c = \frac{1}{4/3} = \frac{3}{4} $$
> > $$ \theta_c = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = 48.59^\circ $$

> [!important] 05: The velocity of light in medium A is $1.414$ times that in medium B, and in medium C it is twice that in medium B. Determine the critical angle at the interface of media A and C.
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > According to the question, 
> > $$ c_A = 1.414 c_B $$
> > $$ c_C = 2 c_B $$
> > 
> > Since $c_C > c_A$, C is the rarer medium and A is the denser medium.
> > Formula for critical angle:
> > $$ \sin \theta_c = \frac{\mu_C}{\mu_A} $$
> > We know that refractive index is inversely proportional to the velocity of light ($\mu \propto \frac{1}{c}$).

> > $$ \sin \theta_c = \frac{c_A}{c_C} = \frac{1.414 c_B}{2 c_B} = \frac{1.414}{2} $$

## Refraction of Light through a Prism
A transparent refracting medium bounded by two inclined plane surfaces is called a prism.

![[assets/prism_refraction.png]] [Diagram: Path of light and angles in a prism]

*   **Refracting Surfaces:** The two plane surfaces inclined to each other through which light enters and leaves the prism are called refracting surfaces.
*   **Angle of the Prism ($A$):** The angle between two adjacent refracting surfaces is called the angle of the prism.
*   **Refracting Edge of the Prism:** The straight line along which the two refracting surfaces meet each other is called the refracting edge of the prism.

**Formulas of a Prism:**
In quadrilateral $AQOR$, 
$\angle A + \angle Q + \angle O + \angle R = 360^\circ$
$\Rightarrow \angle A + \angle O = 180^\circ \dots (i)$ (Since $\angle Q = \angle R = 90^\circ$)

Again, in $\triangle QOR$, 
$r_1 + r_2 + \angle O = 180^\circ \dots (ii)$

From $(i)$ and $(ii)$, we get,
$$ \boxed{\enspace A = r_1 + r_2 \enspace} $$

**Angle of Deviation ($\delta$):**
Since $\delta$ is the exterior angle of $\triangle PQR$,
$$ \delta = (i_1 - r_1) + (i_2 - r_2) $$
$$ \boxed{\enspace \delta = i_1 + i_2 - A \enspace} $$

### Minimum Deviation
As the angle of incidence is gradually increased from a small value, the angle of deviation decreases. Decreasing in this manner, the angle of deviation reaches a minimum value, beyond which any further increase in the angle of incidence causes the angle of deviation to increase instead of decreasing. This minimum value of the angle of deviation is called the **angle of minimum deviation ($\delta_m$)**.

![[assets/min_deviation_graph.png]] [Diagram: Graph of angle of deviation versus angle of incidence]

> [!tip]- Proof (At minimum deviation, $i_1 = i_2$ and $r_1 = r_2$)
> Calculus method:
> We know, $\delta = i_1 + i_2 - A$
> For minimum deviation, $d\delta = 0$,
> $$ d\delta = di_1 + di_2 - 0 = 0 $$
> $$ \Rightarrow di_1 = -di_2 $$
> $$ \Rightarrow \frac{di_1}{di_2} = -1 \dots (1) $$
> 
> Again, $A = r_1 + r_2$
> $$ 0 = dr_1 + dr_2 \Rightarrow dr_1 = -dr_2 \dots (2) $$
> 
> From Snell's law, 
> $$ \sin i_1 = \mu \sin r_1 $$
> Differentiating, we get, $\cos i_1 di_1 = \mu \cos r_1 dr_1 \dots (3)$
> 
> Similarly, for the 2nd surface, 
> $$ \sin i_2 = \mu \sin r_2 $$
> Differentiating, we get, $\cos i_2 di_2 = \mu \cos r_2 dr_2 \dots (4)$
> 
> Dividing equation $(3)$ by $(4)$, we get,
> $$ \frac{\cos i_1 di_1}{\cos i_2 di_2} = \frac{\mu \cos r_1 dr_1}{\mu \cos r_2 dr_2} $$
> 
> Substituting the values (since $di_1 = -di_2$ and $dr_1 = -dr_2$),
> $$ \frac{\cos i_1}{\cos i_2}(-1) = \frac{\cos r_1}{\cos r_2}(-1) $$
> 
> Squaring both sides and solving using trigonometric identities yields,
> $$ r_1 = r_2 $$
> And according to Snell's law, since $r_1 = r_2$, therefore,
> $$ i_1 = i_2 $$

**Formula for Refractive Index at Minimum Deviation:**
Since $i_1 = i_2 = i$ and $r_1 = r_2 = r$,
$$ A = 2r \Rightarrow r = \frac{A}{2} $$
$$ \delta_m = 2i - A \Rightarrow i = \frac{A + \delta_m}{2} $$

Therefore, the refractive index of the material of the prism is,
$$ \mu = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} $$
$$ \boxed{\enspace \mu = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A+\delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)} \enspace} $$

> [!important] 01: There is an equilateral glass prism whose material has a refractive index of $1.5$.
> (i) What is the angle of the prism?
> (ii) What is the angle of minimum deviation of the prism?
> (iii) In the position of minimum deviation, find the angle of incidence at the 1st surface and the angle of emergence at the 2nd surface.
> (iv) In the position of minimum deviation, find the angle of refraction at the 1st surface and the angle of incidence at the 2nd surface.
> (v) In the position of minimum deviation, find the angular deviation of the light ray at the 1st surface.
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > (i) For an equilateral prism, $A = \frac{180^\circ}{3} = 60^\circ$
> > 
> > (ii) We know, $\mu = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A+\delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin(A/2)}$
> > $$ 1.5 = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ+\delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin 30^\circ} $$
> > $$ \sin\left(\frac{60^\circ+\delta_m}{2}\right) = 1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75 $$
> > $$ \frac{60^\circ+\delta_m}{2} = 48.59^\circ $$
> > $$ \delta_m = 37.18^\circ $$
> > 
> > (iii) At minimum deviation, $i_1 = i_2 = \frac{A+\delta_m}{2}$
> > $$ i_1 = i_2 = \frac{60^\circ + 37.18^\circ}{2} = 48.59^\circ $$
> > 
> > (iv) At minimum deviation, $r_1 = r_2 = \frac{A}{2}$
> > $$ r_1 = r_2 = \frac{60^\circ}{2} = 30^\circ $$
> > 
> > (v) Angular deviation at the 1st surface = $i_1 - r_1 = 48.59^\circ - 30^\circ = 18.59^\circ$

> [!important] 02: If the refractive index of the material of a prism is $\sqrt{2}$ and its angle of minimum deviation is $30^\circ$, determine the angle of the prism.
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > $$ \mu = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A+\delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin(A/2)} $$
> > $$ \sqrt{2} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A+30^\circ}{2}\right)}{\sin(A/2)} = \frac{\sin(A/2 + 15^\circ)}{\sin(A/2)} $$
> > $$ \sqrt{2} = \frac{\sin(A/2)\cos 15^\circ + \cos(A/2)\sin 15^\circ}{\sin(A/2)} $$
> > $$ \sqrt{2} = \cos 15^\circ + \cot(A/2)\sin 15^\circ $$
> > $$ \cot(A/2) = \frac{\sqrt{2} - \cos 15^\circ}{\sin 15^\circ} = \sqrt{3} $$
> > $$ \tan(A/2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \Rightarrow A/2 = 30^\circ $$
> > $$ A = 60^\circ $$

> [!important] 03: The refractive index of the material of a prism is $\sqrt{2}$ and the angle of the prism is $60^\circ$. At what angle must a ray of light be incident on the 1st surface of the prism so that it grazes along the 2nd surface upon emergence?
> ![[assets/prism_grazing.png]] [Diagram: Grazing emergence in a prism]
> 
> > [!tip]- Solution
> > Grazing emergence at the 2nd surface means $i_2 = 90^\circ$.
> > Applying Snell's law at the 2nd surface:
> > $$ \mu \sin r_2 = 1 \cdot \sin i_2 $$
> > $$ \sqrt{2} \sin r_2 = 1 \cdot \sin 90^\circ \Rightarrow \sin r_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} $$
> > $$ r_2 = 45^\circ $$
> > 
> > We know, $A = r_1 + r_2$
> > $$ 60^\circ = r_1 + 45^\circ \Rightarrow r_1 = 15^\circ $$
> > 
> > Applying Snell's law at the 1st surface:
> > $$ 1 \cdot \sin i_1 = \mu \sin r_1 $$
> > $$ \sin i_1 = \sqrt{2} \sin 15^\circ $$
> > $$ i_1 = 21.47^\circ $$

### Thin Prism
A prism whose prism angle is between $4^\circ$ and $6^\circ$ is called a thin prism.
When a ray of light is incident on a thin prism at a very small angle with the normal, $\sin \theta \approx \theta$ (in radians).

> [!tip]- Proof
> We know, $\delta = i_1 + i_2 - A$
> 
> According to Snell's law, 
> $$ \mu = \frac{\sin i_1}{\sin r_1} = \frac{\sin i_2}{\sin r_2} $$
> 
> Since the angles are very small, $\sin i_1 \approx i_1$ and $\sin r_1 \approx r_1$
> $$ \therefore \mu = \frac{i_1}{r_1} = \frac{i_2}{r_2} $$
> $$ \Rightarrow i_1 = \mu r_1 \text{ and } i_2 = \mu r_2 $$
> 
> Substituting the values, we get,
> $$ \delta = \mu r_1 + \mu r_2 - A $$
> $$ \delta = \mu (r_1 + r_2) - A $$
> Since $A = r_1 + r_2$,
> $$ \delta = \mu A - A $$
> $$ \boxed{\enspace \delta = A(\mu - 1) \enspace} $$
> *(Note: For a thin prism, the angle of minimum deviation and the angle of deviation are nearly equal)*
