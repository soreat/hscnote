### Niels Bohr's Atomic Model (Postulates)

#### 1. Stationary State Postulate / Orbit Postulate

> The angular momentum of an electron revolving in an orbit of an atom is equal to an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$.

$$\boxed{\enspace L = mv_n r_n = n\frac{h}{2\pi} \enspace}$$

> [!note] Information Related to Angular Momentum
> - $n$ = Principal quantum number (integer, $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$)
> - For the 1st orbit, $L_1 = \frac{h}{2\pi}$
> - For the 2nd orbit, $L_2 = \frac{2h}{2\pi}$

#### 2. Frequency Postulate

> An electron absorbs energy when it jumps from a lower energy level to a higher energy level, and radiates energy when it jumps from a higher energy level to a lower energy level. The amount of absorbed or radiated energy is equal to the energy difference between the two energy levels.

$$\boxed{\enspace E = \Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = hf \enspace}$$

## Determination of Radius and Velocity of the $n$-th Orbit of an Atom

### Radius ($r_n$)

$$\boxed{\enspace r_n = \frac{h^2 \epsilon_0 n^2}{\pi m Z e^2} \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof
> The necessary centripetal force ($F_c$) for an electron revolving in the $n$-th orbit of an atom is provided by the Coulomb attractive force ($F$) between the nucleus (protons) and the electron.
>
> Let,  
> $Z \rightarrow$ Atomic number  
> $e \rightarrow$ Magnitude of charge of a proton or an electron  
> $m \rightarrow$ Mass of an electron  
> $r \rightarrow$ Radius of the orbit  
> $v \rightarrow$ Velocity of the electron
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> F_c = F
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Ze^2}{r^2}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow v^2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Ze^2}{mr}
> \qquad ...\enspace...\enspace (i)
> $$
>
> Again, according to Bohr's postulate,
>
> $$
> mvr = n\frac{h}{2\pi}
> $$
>
> Squaring both sides, we get,
>
> $$
> m^2 v^2 r^2 = \frac{n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2}
> $$
>
> Now, substituting the value of $v^2$ from equation $(i)$, we get,
>
> $$
> m^2 \cdot \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Ze^2}{mr} \cdot r^2 = \frac{n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{m Z e^2 r}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{n^2 h^2}{\pi}
> $$
>
> Therefore, the radius of the $n$-th orbit,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\enspace r_n = \frac{h^2 \epsilon_0 n^2}{\pi m Z e^2} \enspace}
> $$

> [!note] Mnemonic / Trick to Remember
> In these formulas, $h, \epsilon_0, n$ always stay together (**hen** = stays together). Whether in the numerator or the denominator, $h, \epsilon_0, n$ will always be together.

### Velocity ($v_n$)

$$\boxed{\enspace v_n = \frac{Ze^2}{2h\epsilon_0 n} \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof
> From Bohr's angular momentum formula, we get,
>
> $$
> m v_n r_n = n\frac{h}{2\pi}
> $$
>
> Substituting $r_n = \frac{h^2 \epsilon_0 n^2}{\pi m Z e^2}$ here, we get,
>
> $$
> m v_n \cdot \frac{h^2 \epsilon_0 n^2}{\pi m Z e^2} = n\frac{h}{2\pi}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow v_n \cdot \frac{h \epsilon_0 n}{Z e^2} = \frac{1}{2}
> $$
>
> Therefore, the velocity of an electron in the $n$-th orbit,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\enspace v_n = \frac{Ze^2}{2h\epsilon_0 n} \enspace}
> $$

## Total Energy in the $n$-th Orbit of an Atom

$$\boxed{\enspace E_{k_n} = \frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} \enspace}$$
$$\boxed{\enspace E_{p_n} = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{4 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} \enspace}$$
$$\boxed{\enspace E_n = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof
> $\Rightarrow$ **Kinetic Energy ($E_{k_n}$):**
>
> $$
> E_{k_n} = \frac{1}{2} m v_n^2
> = \frac{1}{2} m \left(\frac{Ze^2}{2h\epsilon_0 n}\right)^2
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow E_{k_n} = \frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> $\Rightarrow$ **Potential Energy ($E_{p_n}$):**
>
> If the charge of the nucleus is $Q = +Ze$ and the charge of the electron is $q = -e$, then,
>
> $$
> E_{p_n} = Vq = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Ze}{r_n} (-e)
> = -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_n}
> $$
>
> Substituting $r_n = \frac{h^2 \epsilon_0 n^2}{\pi m Z e^2}$ here,
>
> $$
> E_{p_n} = -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \times \frac{\pi m Z e^2}{h^2 \epsilon_0 n^2}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow E_{p_n} = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{4 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> $\Rightarrow$ **Total Energy ($E_n$):**
>
> $$
> E_n = E_{k_n} + E_{p_n}
> $$
>
> $$
> = \frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} - \frac{m Z^2 e^4}{4 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2}
> $$
>
> $$
> = \frac{m Z^2 e^4}{h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} \left(\frac{1}{8} - \frac{1}{4}\right)
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\enspace E_n = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} \enspace}
> $$

> [!warning] Calculator Warning
> Since the powers of $10$ in the numerator and denominator of the energy formula are very large, inputting all values at once in a calculator may result in a Math Error. Therefore, calculate the rest first and multiply by the value of $m$ afterward.

> [!note] Shortcut Formulas and Energy Relations
> Using the constant values of the 1st orbit of Hydrogen ($Z=1, n=1$), for the $n$-th orbit of any atom:
> - Radius: $r_n = r_{H1} \times \frac{n^2}{Z} = 0.5292 \times \frac{n^2}{Z}\,\text{\AA}$
> - Velocity: $v_n = v_{H1} \times \frac{Z}{n} = 2.18 \times 10^6 \times \frac{Z}{n}\,m\,s^{-1}$
> - Total Energy: $E_n = -\frac{Z^2}{n^2} \times 2.17 \times 10^{-18}\,J = -\frac{Z^2}{n^2} \times 13.6\,eV$
> - Kinetic Energy: $E_{k_n} = +\frac{Z^2}{n^2} \times 2.17 \times 10^{-18}\,J$
> - Potential Energy: $E_{p_n} = -\frac{Z^2}{n^2} \times 4.34 \times 10^{-18}\,J$
> - **Relation:** Total energy and kinetic energy have the same magnitude but opposite signs ($E_n = -E_{k_n}$). Potential energy is negative and twice the total energy ($E_{p_n} = 2E_n = -2E_{k_n}$).
> - **Unit Conversion:** Dividing energy in Joules ($J$) by the charge of an electron ($1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,C$) gives electron volts ($eV$).

> >
> > ---

> > $\Rightarrow$ **Total Energy ($E_{H1}$):**

> > $$
> > E_n = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2}

> > Substituting the values, we get,
> >
> > $$
> > E_{H1} = -\frac{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 1^2 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^4}{8 \times (6.626 \times 10^{-34})^2 \times (8.854 \times 10^{-12})^2 \times 1^2}

> (i) What is the radius of the orbit?  
> (ii) What are the velocity, linear momentum, and angular momentum?  
> (iii) What are the centripetal force and centripetal acceleration?  
> (iv) What are the potential energy, kinetic energy, and total energy?  
> (v) If an electron transitions from the 2nd orbit to the 1st orbit, what are the frequency and wavelength of the emitted photon?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > For Oxygen, atomic number $Z = 8$, and for the 2nd orbit, $n = 2$.
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(i) Radius of the orbit:**
> >
> > $$
> > r_2 = 0.5292 \times 10^{-10} \times \frac{n^2}{Z}
> > = 0.5292 \times 10^{-10} \times \frac{2^2}{8}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 2.646 \times 10^{-11}\,m \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(ii) Velocity, linear momentum, and angular momentum:**
> >
> > Velocity,
> > $$
> > v = 2.18 \times 10^6 \times \frac{Z}{n}
> > = 2.18 \times 10^6 \times \frac{8}{2}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 8.72 \times 10^6\,m\,s^{-1} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Linear momentum,
> > $$
> > P = mv = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 8.72 \times 10^6
> > = \boxed{\enspace 7.94 \times 10^{-24}\,kg\,m\,s^{-1} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Angular momentum,
> > $$
> > L = n\frac{h}{2\pi} = 2 \times \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{2\pi}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 2.11 \times 10^{-34}\,kg\,m^2\,s^{-1} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(iii) Centripetal force and centripetal acceleration:**
> >
> > Centripetal force,
> > $$
> > F = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times (8.72 \times 10^6)^2}{2.646 \times 10^{-11}}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 2.618 \times 10^{-6}\,N \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Centripetal acceleration,
> > $$
> > a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(8.72 \times 10^6)^2}{2.646 \times 10^{-11}}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 2.87 \times 10^{24}\,m\,s^{-2} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(iv) Potential energy, kinetic energy, and total energy:**
> >
> > Kinetic energy,
> > $$
> > E_k = 2.17 \times 10^{-18} \times \frac{Z^2}{n^2}
> > = 2.17 \times 10^{-18} \times \frac{8^2}{2^2}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 3.47 \times 10^{-17}\,J \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Potential energy,
> > $$
> > E_p = -2 E_k = -2 \times 3.47 \times 10^{-17}
> > = \boxed{\enspace -6.94 \times 10^{-17}\,J \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Total energy,
> > $$
> > E = E_k + E_p = -E_k
> > = \boxed{\enspace -3.47 \times 10^{-17}\,J \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(v) Frequency and wavelength of the emitted photon ($n_2 = 2 \rightarrow n_1 = 1$):**
> >
> > We know,
> > $$
> > E = hf = E_2 - E_1 = \frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2} \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
> > $$
> >
> > Using the shortcut formula,
> > $$
> > hf = 2.17 \times 10^{-18} \times Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
> > $$

> > \Rightarrow hf = 2.17 \times 10^{-18} \times 8^2 \left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{2^2}\right)
> > = 1.0416 \times 10^{-16}\,J
> > $$
> >
> > Therefore, frequency,
> > $$
> > f = \frac{1.0416 \times 10^{-16}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 1.572 \times 10^{17}\,Hz \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > And wavelength,
> > $$
> > \lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{1.572 \times 10^{17}}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 1.9 \times 10^{-9}\,m \enspace}
> > $$

> [!important] 03: Photons of wavelengths $2500\,\text{\AA}$ and $4500\,\text{\AA}$ are incident on the 1st orbit of a Hydrogen ($H$) atom respectively. In which case is it possible to eject (remove) the electron from the 1st orbit?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > Total energy of the electron in the 1st orbit of Hydrogen,
> > $$
> > E = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 h^2 \epsilon_0^2 n^2} = -13.6\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > Since the total energy is negative, the electron can only be ejected from the orbit if its total energy is made zero by supplying $+13.6\,eV$ of energy. That is, the minimum energy required to eject the electron is $E_{\text{req}} = +13.6\,eV$.
> >
> > We know, the energy of a photon is $E = hf = h\frac{c}{\lambda}$.
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **1st Case ($\lambda_1 = 2500\,\text{\AA}$):**
> > $$
> > E_1 = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2500 \times 10^{-10}}
> > = 7.9512 \times 10^{-19}\,J
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > = \frac{7.9512 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}\,eV = +4.97\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **2nd Case ($\lambda_2 = 4500\,\text{\AA}$):**
> > $$
> > E_2 = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times \frac{3 \times 10^8}{4500 \times 10^{-10}}
> > = 4.417 \times 10^{-19}\,J = +2.76\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > Since $E_1 < 13.6\,eV$ and $E_2 < 13.6\,eV$, **the electron will not be ejected in either case**.
> >
> > *(Note: If the question asked whether it would jump from the 1st energy level to the 2nd or 3rd energy level, we would have to find $\Delta E = E_2 - E_1$ and compare.)*

> [!important] 04: The ground state energy of a $H$ atom is $-13.6\,eV$. If an electron jumps from the 2nd orbit to the 1st orbit, what is the frequency of the emitted photon?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > Given,  
> > $E_1 = -13.6\,eV$
> >
> > Energy of the 2nd orbit,
> > $$
> > E_2 = E_1 \times \frac{Z^2}{n^2} = -13.6 \times \frac{1^2}{2^2} = -3.4\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > We know, the energy of the emitted photon,
> > $$
> > \Delta E = E_2 - E_1
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow hf = -3.4 - (-13.6) = (-3.4 + 13.6)\,eV = 10.2\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow f = \frac{10.2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 2.46 \times 10^{15}\,Hz \enspace}
> > $$

> [!important] 05: The ground state energy of a $H$ atom is $-13.6\,eV$ and its radius is $0.53\,\text{\AA}$. If an electron in the ground state is struck by a photon of frequency $2.46 \times 10^{15}\,Hz$, will it jump to the 2nd energy level?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > Given,  
> > $E_1 = -13.6\,eV$
> >
> > Energy of the 2nd orbit,
> > $$
> > E_2 = E_1 \times \frac{1^2}{2^2} = -3.4\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > Energy required to be absorbed by the electron to jump from the 1st orbit to the 2nd orbit,
> > $$
> > \Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = -3.4 - (-13.6) = 10.2\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > Now, energy of the incident photon,
> > $$
> > E = hf = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 2.46 \times 10^{15}
> > = 1.63 \times 10^{-18}\,J
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > = \frac{1.63 \times 10^{-18}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}\,eV = 10.2\,eV
> > $$
> >
> > Since the energy of the incident photon $E = \Delta E$, therefore **the electron will jump from the 1st orbit to the 2nd orbit**.

## Nuclear Physics: Radioactive Decay

> Generally, radioactive decay is observed in atoms with an atomic number of **82 or higher**. Inside the nucleus of an atom, similarly charged ($+ve$) protons and neutral neutrons are bound together by the **strong nuclear force**. However, a **Coulomb repulsive force** acts between the protons. In heavy nuclei, when the Coulomb repulsive force exceeds the strong nuclear force, the nucleus breaks apart and transforms into another atom—this is called **radioactive decay**.

### Law of Radioactive Decay (Rutherford and Soddy / Curie)

> The rate of radioactive decay or disintegration of a radioactive element at any instant is directly proportional to the number of undecayed (intact) atoms present at that instant.

$$\boxed{\enspace N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof
> Let,  
> At time $t = 0$, initial number of atoms = $N_0$  
> At time $t = t$, number of undecayed atoms = $N$  
> $\lambda \rightarrow$ Decay constant or Radioactive decay constant
>
> According to the law of radioactive decay,
> $$
> \frac{dN}{dt} \propto -N
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N
> $$
>
> *(Note: The negative sign `(-)` is used because the number of atoms decreases with time.)*
>
> Separating the variables and integrating, we get,
> $$
> \int_{N_0}^{N} \frac{dN}{N} = \int_{0}^{t} -\lambda\,dt
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow [\ln N]_{N_0}^{N} = -\lambda [t]_0^t
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \ln \frac{N}{N_0} = -\lambda t
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{N}{N_0} = e^{-\lambda t}
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \boxed{\enspace N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} \enspace}
> $$

[Figure: Exponential decay graph of $N$ vs $t$ (of the form $y = me^{-nx}$)]

### Decay Constant ($\lambda$)

> 1. The ratio of the rate of decay of a radioactive element to the number of undecayed atoms present at that instant is called the **decay constant** of that element: $\lambda = \frac{|dN/dt|}{N}$.
> 2. The reciprocal of the time in which **37%** of the atoms of a radioactive element remain undecayed is called the decay constant.

> [!tip]- Proof (37% Definition)
> Let, $t = \frac{1}{\lambda}$. Then,
> $$
> N = N_0 e^{-\lambda \cdot \frac{1}{\lambda}} = \frac{N_0}{e} = \frac{N_0}{2.718} \approx 0.3679 N_0 \approx 0.37 N_0 = 37\% \text{ of } N_0
> $$

### Half-life ($T_{1/2}$)

> The time required for half of the total number of atoms of a radioactive element to decay (or for half to remain undecayed) is called the **half-life ($T_{1/2}$)** of the element.

$$\boxed{\enspace T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} \enspace}$$

> [!tip]- Proof
> At time $t = T_{1/2}$, the number of undecayed atoms is $N = \frac{N_0}{2}$.
>
> $$
> N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Rightarrow \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{e^{\lambda T_{1/2}}}
> \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> e^{\lambda T_{1/2}} = 2
> $$
>
> Taking $\ln$ on both sides, we get,
> $$
> \lambda T_{1/2} = \ln 2
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \boxed{\enspace T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} \enspace}
> $$

### Mean Life / Average Life ($\tau$)

> The sum of the lifetimes of all the atoms of a radioactive element divided by the initial total number of atoms ($N_0$) is called the **mean life ($\tau$)**.

$$\boxed{\enspace \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} \enspace}$$

### Units of Radioactivity and Other Forms

> [!note] Units of Radioactivity
> - **Becquerel ($Bq$, SI unit):** $1$ disintegration or decay per second of a radioactive element is called $1\,\text{Bq}$.
> - **Curie ($\text{Curie}$):** $3.7 \times 10^{10}$ disintegrations per second is called $1\,\text{Curie}$.
>   - $1\,\text{Curie} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\,\text{Bq}$
>   - $1\,\text{milliCurie (mCi)} = 3.7 \times 10^7\,\text{Bq}$
>   - $1\,\text{microCurie (}\mu\text{Ci)} = 3.7 \times 10^4\,\text{Bq}$

> [!note] Other Forms of the Radioactive Decay Law
> Along with the number of atoms ($N = n N_A = \frac{m}{M} N_A$), the same exponential formula applies to the number of moles, mass, and activity:
> - **Number of moles:** $n = n_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> - **Mass:** $m = m_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> - **Activity / Rate of disintegration ($A = \frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N$):** $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ or $\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{dN_0}{dt} e^{-\lambda t}$

> [!important] 06: The half-life of Radon is $3.82\,\text{days}$.  
> (i) In what time will $30\%$ of its initial number of atoms decay?  
> (ii) In what time will $30\%$ of its initial number of atoms remain undecayed?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > Given, $T_{1/2} = 3.82\,\text{days}$.
> >
> > Therefore, decay constant,
> > $$
> > \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{3.82} = 0.18145\,\text{day}^{-1}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(i) When $30\%$ decays:**
> >
> > Number of undecayed atoms, $N = 70\% \text{ of } N_0 = 0.7 N_0$.
> >
> > $$
> > 0.7 N_0 = N_0 e^{-0.18145 t}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow \ln(0.7) = -0.18145 t
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow t = \frac{\ln(0.7)}{-0.18145} = \boxed{\enspace 1.9657\,\text{days} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(ii) When $30\%$ remains undecayed:**
> >
> > Number of undecayed atoms, $N = 0.3 N_0$.
> >
> > $$
> > 0.3 N_0 = N_0 e^{-0.18145 t}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow t = \frac{\ln(0.3)}{-0.18145} = \boxed{\enspace 6.6353\,\text{days} \enspace}
> > $$

> [!important] 07: The sum of the half-lives of two radioactive elements $A$ and $B$ is $15$ years, and the half-life of $A$ is twice that of $B$.  
> (i) Determine the decay constant and mean life of $A$.  
> (ii) It takes different amounts of time for $40\%$ of both elements to decay—analyze mathematically.
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > Given,  
> > $T_{1/2 A} + T_{1/2 B} = 15\,\text{years} \qquad ...\enspace...\enspace (i)$  
> > $T_{1/2 A} = 2 T_{1/2 B}$
> >
> > From equation $(i)$, we get,
> > $$
> > 2 T_{1/2 B} + T_{1/2 B} = 15
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > T_{1/2 B} = 5\,\text{years}
> > $$
> >
> > Therefore, $T_{1/2 A} = 2 \times 5 = 10\,\text{years}$.
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(i) Decay constant and mean life of $A$:**
> >
> > Decay constant,
> > $$
> > \lambda_A = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2 A}} = \frac{0.693}{10} = \boxed{\enspace 0.0693\,\text{year}^{-1} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Mean life,
> > $$
> > \tau_A = \frac{1}{\lambda_A} = \frac{1}{0.0693} = \boxed{\enspace 14.43\,\text{years} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(ii) Comparison of time required for $40\%$ decay:**
> >
> > When $40\%$ decays, the undecayed portion is $N = 0.6 N_0$.
> >
> > **For element $A$:**
> > $$
> > 0.6 N_0 = N_0 e^{-\lambda_A t_A}
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > \ln(0.6) = -0.0693 t_A
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow t_A = \frac{\ln(0.6)}{-0.0693} = 7.37\,\text{years}
> > $$
> >
> > **For element $B$:**
> > $$
> > \lambda_B = \frac{0.693}{5}\,\text{year}^{-1}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > 0.6 N_0 = N_0 e^{-\lambda_B t_B}
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > \ln(0.6) = -\left(\frac{0.693}{5}\right) t_B
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow t_B = 3.685\,\text{years}
> > $$
> >
> > Since $t_A \neq t_B$, therefore it takes different amounts of time for $40\%$ of both elements to decay.

> [!important] 08: It takes $10$ days for $30\%$ of a radioactive element to decay.  
> (i) How many days will it take for $70\%$ of that element to decay?  
> (ii) In what time will the element decay completely?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > When $30\%$ decays, the undecayed portion is $N = 0.7 N_0$.
> >
> > $$
> > 0.7 N_0 = N_0 e^{-\lambda \times 10}
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > \lambda = \frac{\ln(0.7)}{-10}\,\text{day}^{-1}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(i) When $70\%$ decays:**
> >
> > Undecayed portion is $N = 0.3 N_0$.
> >
> > $$
> > 0.3 N_0 = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > t = \frac{\ln(0.3)}{-\lambda}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > = \ln(0.3) \times \frac{10}{\ln(0.7)} = \boxed{\enspace 33.7554\,\text{days} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(ii) Time required for complete decay:**
> >
> > For complete decay, the number of undecayed atoms $N = 0$.
> >
> > $$
> > 0 = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > \frac{1}{e^{\lambda t}} = 0
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > e^{\lambda t} = \infty
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow \boxed{\enspace t = \infty \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Therefore, it will take **infinite time** for a radioactive element to decay completely.

> [!important] 09: A piece of wood was found during an excavation at Mahasthangarh. Upon testing in a laboratory, it was found that $^{14}C$ present in it undergoes $360$ radioactive decays per hour. The initial activity of $^{14}C$ is $936$ per hour and its half-life is $5570\,\text{yr}$.  
> (i) What are the mean life and decay constant?  
> (ii) Determine the age of the Mahasthangarh civilization from this data.
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(i) Decay constant and mean life:**
> >
> > Decay constant,
> > $$
> > \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{\ln 2}{5570} = \boxed{\enspace 1.24443 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{yr}^{-1} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > Mean life,
> > $$
> > \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{1.24443 \times 10^{-4}} = \boxed{\enspace 8035.81\,\text{yr} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(ii) Age of the Mahasthangarh civilization:**
> >
> > We know,
> > $$
> > \frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{dN_0}{dt} e^{-\lambda t}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow 360 = 936 \times e^{-1.24443 \times 10^{-4} t}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow \ln\left(\frac{360}{936}\right) = -1.24443 \times 10^{-4} t
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow t = \boxed{\enspace 7678.30\,\text{yr} \enspace}
> > $$

> [!important] 10: The mean life of element $X$ is $760\,\text{yr}$. According to the graph, the number of atoms of element $Y$ becomes $\frac{N_0}{3}$ in $100$ years.  
> [Figure: $N$ vs $t$ graph for element $Y$ where $N = N_0/3$ at $t = 100$ years]  
> (i) What is the half-life of element $X$?  
> (ii) Are the mean lives and half-lives of elements $X$ and $Y$ equal?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(i) Half-life of element $X$:**
> >
> > Given, $\tau_x = \frac{1}{\lambda_x} = 760\,\text{yr}$.
> >
> > $$
> > T_{x 1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda_x} = \ln(2) \times \tau_x = \ln(2) \times 760 = \boxed{\enspace 526.79\,\text{yr} \enspace}
> > $$
> >
> > ---
> >
> > $\Rightarrow$ **(ii) Comparison of mean life and half-life of $X$ and $Y$:**
> >
> > For element $Y$, at $t = 100\,\text{yr}$, $N = \frac{N_0}{3}$.
> >
> > $$
> > \frac{N_0}{3} = N_0 e^{-100 \lambda_y}
> > \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
> > \lambda_y = \frac{\ln(1/3)}{-100}
> > $$
> >
> > Therefore, mean life of $Y$,
> > $$
> > \tau_y = \frac{1}{\lambda_y} = \frac{-100}{\ln(1/3)} = 91.02\,\text{yr}
> > $$
> >
> > And half-life of $Y$,
> > $$
> > T_{y 1/2} = \ln(2) \times \tau_y = \ln(2) \times 91.02 = 63.093\,\text{yr}
> > $$
> >
> > Since $\tau_x \neq \tau_y$ and $T_{x 1/2} \neq T_{y 1/2}$, therefore the mean lives and half-lives of elements $X$ and $Y$ are **not equal**.

> [!important] 11: The half-life of Radium ($^{226}Ra$) is $1620\,\text{years}$. How many atoms will disintegrate per second from $1\,g$ of it?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > We know,
> > $$
> > \frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} \times \frac{m \cdot N_A}{M}
> > $$
> >
> > Here,  
> > $m = 1\,g$  
> > $M = 226\,g\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ *(Since $M$ is gram molecular/atomic mass, $m$ must also be in grams)*  
> > $N_A = 6.023 \times 10^{23}$  
> > $T_{1/2} = 1620 \times 365 \times 24 \times 3600\,s$
> >
> > Substituting the values, we get,
> > $$
> > \frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{\ln(2) \times 1 \times 6.023 \times 10^{23}}{1620 \times 365 \times 24 \times 3600 \times 226}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 3.61 \times 10^{10}\,\text{disintegrations/second} \enspace}
> > $$

> [!important] 12: The half-life of $^{224}Pb$ is $26.8\,\text{min}$. What mass of it will yield a radioactivity of $1\,\text{Curie}$?
>
> > [!tip]- Solution
> >
> > Given,  
> > $\frac{dN}{dt} = 1\,\text{Curie} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\,\text{Bq}$  
> > $T_{1/2} = 26.8 \times 60\,s$  
> > $M = 224\,g\,\text{mol}^{-1}$
> >
> > We know,
> > $$
> > \frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} \left(\frac{m \cdot N_A}{M}\right)
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > \Rightarrow m = \frac{dN}{dt} \times \frac{T_{1/2} \cdot M}{\ln(2) \cdot N_A}
> > $$
> >
> > $$
> > = \frac{3.7 \times 10^{10} \times 26.8 \times 60 \times 224}{\ln(2) \times 6.023 \times 10^{23}}
> > = \boxed{\enspace 3.19 \times 10^{-8}\,g \enspace}
> > $$

## Why Does Radioactive Decay Occur?

[Figure: Action of strong nuclear force ($F$) and Coulomb repulsive force ($F_e$) in a small nucleus vs a large nucleus]

> - **Strong nuclear force:** It is a *short-range force* (it does not attract beyond a certain distance).
> - **Coulomb repulsive force:** It is a *long-range force* (it repels even from a distance).
>
> In a small nucleus, although all protons repel each other, all neutrons and protons attract each other. As a result, the attractive nuclear force is dominant and radioactivity is not observed. However, in a large nucleus, while distant protons still repel each other, distant neutrons cannot attract them. Consequently, when the repulsive force becomes greater, protons are ejected and radioactive decay occurs.
>
> That is why, although the number of neutrons and protons is nearly equal in smaller atoms, heavy atoms contain many more neutrons than protons to remain stable. Generally, radioactive decay occurs in atoms with atomic number **82 or heavier**.

## Mass Defect and Binding Energy

Symbol of an atom: ${}^A_Z X$, where:
- $A$ = Mass number or Nucleon number ($P + n$)
- $Z$ = Atomic number or Proton number ($P$)
- Number of neutrons, $n = A - Z$

### Mass Defect ($\Delta m$)

> The actual mass ($m_N$) of a combined nucleus is slightly less than the sum of the masses of its constituent protons and neutrons in their free (isolated) state. This difference in mass is called the **mass defect ($\Delta m$)**.

$$\boxed{\enspace \Delta m = Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n - m_N \enspace}$$

### Binding Energy ($B.E$) and Average Binding Energy

> The energy released when free protons and neutrons combine to form a stable nucleus (or the energy required to break a nucleus apart into free protons and neutrons) is called **binding energy**. According to Einstein's equation $E = mc^2$, the mass defect $\Delta m$ is converted into this energy.

$$\boxed{\enspace B.E = \Delta m c^2 \enspace}$$
$$\boxed{\enspace \text{Average Binding Energy (Per nucleon energy)} = \frac{B.E}{A} \enspace}$$

> [!note] Necessary Constants and Information
> - $1\,\text{amu} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}\,kg$
> - Dividing mass in $kg$ by $1.660539 \times 10^{-27}\,kg$ gives mass in $\text{amu}$.
> - Calculations from mass defect show that approximately **$200\,MeV$** of energy is released in the fission reaction of a single Uranium nucleus.
> - *(Read other theoretical topics including the properties of $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ rays from the textbook.)*
